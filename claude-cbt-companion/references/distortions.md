# Cognitive distortions — reference

Burns' classic list (from *The Feeling Good Handbook*, as used in the
Cognivia dataset). Load this when deciding whether Structured Mode applies,
or when you want a concrete example of a pattern before naming it.

When unsure whether a distortion is present, stay in Natural Mode — never
name a pattern you're not confident in.

Each entry: definition, one or two example thoughts, and an example
rational response (counter-thought in the person's own voice — this is the
tone to aim for in reframes, not a lecture).

## 1. All-or-nothing thinking

You look at things in absolute, black-and-white categories.

- Thought: "I didn't do an excellent job on the paper."
  Rational response: "The paper is pretty good even though it's not perfect."
- Thought: "If I'm not 'the greatest,' it means I won't get any attention from people."
  Rational response: "Whether or not I'm 'the greatest,' people will listen, they will see me perform, and many will respond positively."

## 2. Overgeneralization

You view a negative event as a never-ending pattern of defeat.

- Thought: "I never do anything right."
  Rational response: "I do some things right and some wrong, just like everyone else. I fouled up on my appointment, but let's not blow this out of proportion."
- Thought: "This means I'll never get a job."
  Rational response: "I can test this by applying for a series of other jobs and putting my best foot forward to see what happens."

## 3. Mental filter

You dwell on the negatives.

- Thought: "The professor will notice all the typos and the weak sections."
  Rational response: "He probably will notice typos, but he'll read the whole paper. There are some fairly good sections."

## 4. Discounting the positives

You insist that your accomplishments or positive qualities don't count.

- Thought: "Not one person on earth gives a damn about me."
  Rational response: "When she was discharged from the hospital, many patients and staff expressed great fondness for her." (Note: this one is evidence-gathering — the exercise is recalling what actually happened against the claim.)

## 5. Mind reading

You assume people are reacting negatively to you without definite evidence.

- Thought: "This audience thinks I'm a bore."
  Rational response: "You don't actually know what they think — what evidence do you have?" (The dataset's responses here are weak; the move is asking for evidence, not asserting the opposite.)

## 6. Fortune telling

You arbitrarily predict things will turn out badly.

- Thought: "I'll probably flub typing this and make a bunch of typos."
  Rational response: "I don't have to type perfectly. I can correct the errors."

## 7. Magnification or minimization

You blow things out of proportion or shrink their importance.

- Thought: "He acted like a pain in the butt — I'd like to strangle him."
  Rational response: "He acted like a pain in the butt. We all do at times. Why let this get to me?"

## 8. Emotional reasoning

You reason from how you feel: "I feel like an idiot, so I really must be one."

- Thought: "I feel like a dud, therefore I am a dud."
  Rational response: "Your feelings reflect your thoughts and beliefs. If the thoughts are distorted, the emotions have no validity — feeling like a dud is a symptom of the thought, not proof of it."

## 9. Should statements

You criticize yourself (or others) with "shoulds," "oughts," "musts," "have tos."

- Thought: "I shouldn't be feeling so nervous since everything is going well for me."
  Rational response: "Human beings are not perfectly rational. Some people do get nervous even when things are going well."

## 10. Labeling

Instead of "I made a mistake," you tell yourself "I'm a jerk," "a fool," "a loser."

- Thought: "I am a loser."
  Rational response: "I've had some degree of success. We've reared three fine children. People admire and respect me. One bad outcome doesn't define me."

## 11. Personalization and blame

You blame yourself for something you weren't entirely responsible for, or blame others and deny your own role.

- Thought: "If Luci gets a sore throat, it will be my fault."
  Rational response: "I made an honest mistake. She probably won't get a sore throat. If she does, it won't be the end of the world, and I don't deserve to be punished like this."
